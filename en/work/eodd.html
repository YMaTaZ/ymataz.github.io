01Context · Constraints

The problem is not "the explanation is unclear", it is "nobody will read it through"

In high-stakes settings, several agents reason in parallel and a person confirms the last step. The system has to be able to state its own reasoning — and that chain often runs to twenty-odd steps.

The conventional approach is to add an explanation: lay the chain out underneath and let the user read. In usability testing, nobody read it. The real problem is not a lack of information, it is that nobody will read it through.

What I had to build was not a more detailed explanation. It was letting a person jump to a step instead of reading in order.

Three constraints

  • The decision must be readable. You cannot ask someone to read the whole chain; that is the same as no explanation.
  • It must be attributable. Who rejected which step has to be on record.
  • It must not add mental load. More explanation, without more effort — this was the one I worried about most.

02Decision 1 / 3

Explainable does not mean "give more information"

The situation
The system's reasoning ran to more than twenty steps
What I assumed
Show all of it, and people will trust it
What was actually true
Fully expanded, nobody read it; what people want is to jump to a step
So I changed
The trace became a tree that expands, can be flagged and can be rejected
What it cost
"Which step am I on" had to be designed separately, or people get lost in the tree

The tree structure is not the point. The point is letting someone ask the question that actually matters: "this is the step that is wrong" — then jump straight to it instead of reading from the top.

From an interview
"I cannot read twenty steps in five minutes."
Rejected because of it
An overview page, and the first version that laid the whole reasoning chain out
03Decision 2 / 3

There is no such state as "partly believe"

The situation
Users sat between "believe all of it" and "do not use it"
What I assumed
There would be a middle state of partial belief
What was actually true
There is no middle state: people will not make an important decision with something they cannot review
So I changed
Every conclusion carries its evidence; no evidence, no conclusion
What it cost
The system has to keep citations at generation time, moving cost to engineering

That decision turned a design problem into an engineering one: if the interface requires every conclusion to open onto evidence, the system has to retain citations at generation. It cannot be patched in afterwards.

04Decision 3 / 3

More explanation must not make people more tired

This is the only place in the book where a design assumption was tested by experiment — and what it tested was the thing I myself worried about: with this much explanation added, does cognitive load go up?

The situation
Concern that added explanation would raise cognitive load
What I assumed
The more you look at, the more tired you get
What was actually true
p = .37: mental demand did not increase significantly (N=16)
So I changed
Kept the full trace, did not trim it to "save effort"
What it cost
The interface is dense, and density has to be controlled by structure rather than whitespace

The four required fields of a decision trace

aRationale LogThe reasoning record for each step: evidence, judgement, conclusion.
bRole ContributionsWhich agent contributed what, attributable to a specific role.
cAssumptionsAssumptions written down explicitly, each one judgeable as valid or not.
dConfidence ScoresEach agent's self-rated confidence in its own conclusion.
